Should we spend $30,000 to fix trucks we aren't using?

Deciding whether a trucking company should repair 15 idle tractors instead of paying spot-market rates.

Fifteen trucks sat in maintenance while overflow freight went out at three dollars a mile or more. The company's own trucks cost under a dollar.

The short answer

Yes. Every load handed to the spot market costs about three times more per mile. Repairing all 15 idle trucks adds about $51,000 of profit in a single week.

  • It pays back almost immediately. Each repaired truck earns back its $2,000 in roughly 870 miles, one or two loads.
  • The case is robust. It holds unless the repair quote passes about $5,400 per truck, and under 20% swings in spot prices.
  • Keep the model running. Assigning loads with it daily keeps spot-market use, and cost, down.

The approach

We set the problem up as a mixed-integer optimization. Each of the week's 377 loads goes either to a specific truck or to the spot market; every load must be covered, no truck carries two loads on the same day, and an idle truck can only be used if its repair is paid. The model chooses the most profitable plan, including which trucks are worth repairing.

What we recommended

Approve the $30,000 repair budget, return all 15 trucks to the dispatch pool, and use the model for daily load assignment. Then track whether repaired trucks pass break-even, roughly 870 miles each, and consider buying more tractors if spot-market use stays high.

Limits

One representative week of data. Trucks are assumed able to start anywhere, so empty repositioning miles are not charged, and repaired trucks are assumed to stay in service with drivers available. Real savings will be somewhat lower.
